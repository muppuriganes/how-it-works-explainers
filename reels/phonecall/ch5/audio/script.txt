Now the voice packets can flow. From the tower, they don't stay in the air for long. Most towers are connected by fibre optic cables. These are glass threads, thinner than a human hair, that carry data as flashes of light. The light bounces along inside the glass, even around bends, for thousands of kilometres. Along the way, machines called routers read the address on each packet, and send it onward, hop by hop. To cross an ocean, your call doesn't go to space. It dives under the sea. More than ninety five percent of the data between continents travels through cables lying on the ocean floor. There are hundreds of them, more than a million kilometres in total. That's enough to wrap around the Earth thirty times. A call from India to America might travel through the Arabian Sea, past Egypt, across the Mediterranean, and under the Atlantic. Inside the glass, light moves at about two hundred thousand kilometres per second, so even this long trip takes only about a tenth of a second. Satellites can carry calls too, but they orbit thirty six thousand kilometres above us. Going up and coming back down adds about a quarter of a second each way. That's why cables win.
