# How a food delivery order reaches you in 30 minutes

Structure follows the concept: an order goes through 5 real tracker stages, so the video has 5 stops.
Order placed → Restaurant accepts → Rider assigned → On the way → Delivered.
Chapters change with a push-notification wipe. Running joke: "Your rider is 2 minutes away."
The visuals are generic and unbranded. Narration names the apps only as facts.

## Stop 1 · Order placed
It's ten at night. You're hungry. You tap one button, and thirty minutes later, a biryani shows up at your door. Like magic. Except, it's not magic. It's a restaurant kitchen, a bunch of prediction models, and one rider on a scooter, racing a traffic signal. In India, apps like Swiggy and Zomato deliver millions of food orders every single day. So tonight, we follow one order. One biryani. Five stops on the order tracker, from your tap to your door. Oh, and at some point, the app will tell you: your rider is two minutes away. Start a timer. Order placed.

## Stop 2 · Restaurant accepts
The moment you pay, your order flies to a tablet in the restaurant kitchen. It beeps. Loudly. Until someone taps accept. Now the app has to make a guess: how long will this biryani take? That guess is called food preparation time, and a machine learning model makes it. It looks at what you ordered, how many items, and how busy that kitchen is right now. One biryani on a Tuesday afternoon? Quick. Forty orders on a Friday night? Good luck. And this guess matters more than anything. Because it decides exactly when to send your rider.

## Stop 3 · Rider assigned
Here's the clever bit. The app does not grab the nearest rider the second you order. Too early, and the rider stands around the kitchen, waiting. Too late, and your biryani sits on the counter, going cold. So the app times it. It's called just in time assignment: the rider should walk in right as the food is packed. Think of it as two races running at once. Race one, the kitchen cooking. Race two, the rider riding to the restaurant. Your wait is whichever race is slower, plus the ride to your door. And if two orders are going the same way? One rider carries both. That's called batching.

## Stop 4 · On the way
Food packed. Bag zipped. Picked up! Now that tiny scooter on your screen starts moving, and that part is real. The rider's phone keeps sending its GPS location, and the app predicts the arrival time using the roads, live traffic, and thousands of past trips. Then comes the last mile, where the plan meets reality. A red signal. A cow, having a meeting in the middle of the road. And the hardest part of the whole trip: the last hundred metres. The security gate. And an address that says: Tower B, behind the temple, near the big tree. Meanwhile, on your screen: your rider is two minutes away. Still. It has been nine minutes.

## Stop 5 · Delivered
Ding dong. Delivered. Now, where did your three hundred rupees go? The restaurant pays the app a commission, often fifteen to thirty percent. You pay a platform fee, around fifteen to eighteen rupees, plus delivery charges and taxes. The rider? Reports say roughly fifty to seventy rupees an order, and critics say often less. Tips? Only three to five percent of orders get one. So, maybe tip. That's the real trick. Not one magic algorithm, but lots of small predictions, all trying to be right at the same moment. And as you open the bag, your phone buzzes one last time. Your rider is two minutes away.
