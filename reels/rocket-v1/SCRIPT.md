# How a rocket actually reaches space: launch pad to orbit and back

Five phases, because that is how a real mission is split: countdown, liftoff, staging, orbit, return.
Tone: funny, interactive, viewer in the story ("you're the one pressing the button"). Quick-guess quiz in most phases.
Theme: mission control. Chrome = mission patch + a "T+" mission clock with telemetry (altitude, speed, g-force).
Chapter change = a countdown. The next phase is a flipping T-minus number, and a launch-smoke wipe opens on it.
Running joke: the "rocket science" cliché, and a very tired flight director who has had too much chai.
Voice: Kokoro `af_heart`. Host: the simple girl host. Rocket: a generic, unbranded two-stage launcher with a crew capsule (not any real company's vehicle).

Facts to keep honest (rounded, hedged where needed):
- A launcher is roughly 90% propellant by weight. The structure and payload are a tiny slice.
- At liftoff, thrust has to be bigger than weight. Rockets do not "push off the ground". They push exhaust down, and the exhaust pushes back.
- Sound-suppression water on the pad is real: it takes the edge off the rocket's own noise.
- Max-Q is the moment of peak aerodynamic pressure, about a minute or so after liftoff. The engines often throttle down to get through it.
- The rocket does not fly straight up. It tips over gradually (the gravity turn), because orbit is mostly sideways speed.
- Stage separation happens a couple of minutes after liftoff, when the first stage is nearly empty. Dropping dead weight is the whole point of staging.
- Low Earth orbit needs about 28,000 km/h (about 7.8 km/s). The ISS is roughly 400 km up and goes round about every 90 minutes, so the crew sees around 16 sunrises a day.
- Weightlessness is free fall: the crew and the station are falling together, and missing the Earth.
- Re-entry: the capsule hits the air at orbital speed, and the heat shield takes temperatures of around 1,500 °C or more. It is compression of the air, not friction.
- Return finishes under parachutes, ending in a splashdown (or a landing).
- India: ISRO. Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south pole in August 2023, and Gaganyaan is India's crewed mission programme. No claims about specific dates.

## Phase 1 · Countdown
Okay. You're sitting at a desk in Mission Control, and in front of you is a very big, very red button. No pressure. Behind that button, a tower, a rocket, and about nine tenths of it is just fuel. Ninety percent. The rocket is basically a giant flying petrol tank with a tiny, very brave, passenger seat on top. Five phases. Countdown, liftoff, staging, orbit, and return. But first, a quick guess. How much of this thing is actually fuel? A, a quarter. B, half. Or C, nearly all of it. Lock your answer. It's C. About ninety percent. Everything else, the engines, the tanks, the capsule, the astronauts, is the leftover. Now, ten, nine, eight. Before the engines start, a flood of water gets dumped on the pad, so the rocket's own noise doesn't shake it to bits. Seven, six, five. Ignition. And somewhere in the back row of mission control, a very tired flight director takes a very long sip of chai. Next up, liftoff.

## Phase 2 · Liftoff
Phase two. Liftoff. The engines roar, but the rocket doesn't move. Why? Because thrust has to beat weight first. When it finally does, up she goes. And no, the rocket does not push against the ground. It throws burning gas downwards, and the gas pushes the rocket back. Same trick as letting go of a balloon. Except this balloon weighs five hundred tonnes. Then the rocket does something odd. It starts to lean over. Gently. Because getting to space is the easy part. Staying there needs sideways speed. About a minute in comes Max Q. The moment the air pushes back hardest. So the engines ease off for a moment, to get through it. Then back on full. Meanwhile, the astronauts feel their weight go up. Three times heavier. Quick, how does it feel? Like three of your cousins sitting on your chest. Next up, staging.

## Phase 3 · Staging
Phase three. Staging. After about two and a half minutes, the first stage is nearly empty. And an empty fuel tank is just very expensive dead weight. So, the rocket drops it. Bang. The first stage falls away, the second stage lights up, and the fairing opens like a clamshell, to let the satellite or capsule see the sky. Quick guess. What happens to the dropped stage? Does it, A, fall into the ocean and sink. B, burn up. Or C, fly back, turn around, and land on its tail. Plot twist. Some of them, C. Landing legs out, engines relit, and a boat in the ocean waiting, or a landing pad. Reusable rockets are why spaceflight is getting cheaper. Dropping weight is the whole trick. Every kilo you don't carry is a kilo you don't have to burn fuel to lift. Next up, orbit.

## Phase 4 · Orbit
Phase four. Orbit. And here's the big secret. Orbit isn't floating. Orbit is falling. A cannonball, fired sideways from a very tall mountain, falls to the ground. Fire it faster, it lands farther. Fire it fast enough, and the ground curves away just as fast as the ball falls. It keeps falling, and keeps missing. That speed is about twenty eight thousand kilometres an hour. Quick guess. How long does one lap around the Earth take at that speed? Ten minutes? Ninety minutes? Or a whole day? Ninety minutes. So the crew sees about sixteen sunrises every single day. And the crew floats, because they, and the whole station, are falling together. Weightless, doesn't mean gravity is gone. It means nothing is holding you up. And somewhere down there, it's your cousin's wedding. Next up, return.

## Phase 5 · Return
Phase five. Return. Coming back is the hardest part. The capsule fires its engines, slows down, and drops into the air at twenty eight thousand kilometres an hour. And the air in front of it gets squashed so hard, it glows. Plasma. Around fifteen hundred degrees. That's not friction, that's compression. So the heat shield takes the heat, and gets sacrificed, bit by bit. Then comes the quiet bit. Small parachutes, then big ones. Slower, slower, and a gentle splash in the ocean. Welcome home. The recovery boat arrives, the door opens, and the first thing the astronaut asks for is, chai. Of course. So. Countdown, liftoff, staging, orbit, return. That's how nine tenths fuel, one tiny capsule, and a very brave tired flight director actually reach space. And back. Please remain seated. We are, technically, still falling.
