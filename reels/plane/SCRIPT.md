# How a plane actually flies — runway to 35,000 ft and back

Structure follows the concept: a flight has 5 real phases.
Take-off → climb → cruise → descent → landing.
Theme: a boarding pass + flight tracker. Chapter change = the window shade slides down, then opens on the next phase's view.
Running joke: the uncle in row 23 who stands up the second the wheels touch down.
Voice: Kokoro `af_heart`. Host: the simple girl host. Plane: a generic, unbranded twin-jet airliner (A320-class).

## Phase 1 · Take-off
Seventy tonnes of metal, fuel and people, sitting at the end of a runway. In a minute, it will be flying. In India alone, around three thousand domestic flights take off every single day. So let's ride one, all the way up and back down. Five phases. Take-off, climb, cruise, descent, and landing. Engines to full power. The plane races down the runway, faster and faster, until, at around two hundred and sixty kilometres an hour, the pilot gently pulls back. The nose lifts. That moment is called rotation. And the wings take over. Oh, and somewhere in row twenty three, an uncle is already planning to stand up at landing. Remember him.

## Phase 2 · Climb
So how do wings lift seventy tonnes? A wing is shaped and tilted so it pushes air downward. Push air down, and the air pushes the wing up. That's lift. More speed, more lift. And the speed comes from the engines. Inside each one, a giant fan sucks in air. A small part goes into the core, where fuel burns and spins the turbines. But most of the air flows around the core, and that bypass air makes about eighty percent of the push. Meanwhile, the cabin pressure slowly drops, and your ears go pop.

## Phase 3 · Cruise
Thirty five thousand feet. Higher than Mount Everest. Outside, it's around minus fifty degrees, and the air is too thin to breathe. So the cabin is pumped up, to feel like a hill station, around six to eight thousand feet. Why fly so high? Thin air means less drag, so the plane burns less fuel, cruising at about eight hundred and thirty kilometres an hour. Most of this is flown by the autopilot, along invisible highways in the sky, while the pilots watch, check, and talk to air traffic control. And no, they are not sleeping.

## Phase 4 · Descent
About two hundred kilometres before the airport, the plane starts coming down. Pilots use a simple rule: three nautical miles for every thousand feet. Air traffic control lines up the arrivals like a queue at a ticket counter, one plane every few minutes. Close to the runway, an invisible radio beam called ILS draws a perfect three degree slope, and the plane slides down it, like a ball on a ramp. Flaps slide out of the wings, for more lift at low speed. Landing gear down. Clunk.

## Phase 5 · Landing
At around two hundred and fifty kilometres an hour, the main wheels touch the runway. Thump. Spoilers pop up to kill the lift. The engines roar, with reverse thrust pushing air forward. And the brakes do the rest. Seventy tonnes, stopped in well under a minute. And right on cue, our uncle in row twenty three is up, bag in hand, while the crew says: please remain seated. Uncle, the plane is still moving. Take-off, climb, cruise, descent, landing. That's how a plane really flies.
