# How India's bullet train works — narration

Voice: Kokoro `am_puck`. Each coach is one chapter.

## Coach 1

Mumbai to Ahmedabad. Five hundred and eight kilometres. By car? About eight hours, three toll plazas, and one fight about the AC. By India's bullet train, opening from twenty twenty seven? About two hours. That's shorter than one cricket innings. So today, we're taking a ride. Seven coaches. Seven secrets. Each coach explains one trick that lets a train cruise at three hundred and twenty kilometres an hour. And to prove how smooth it is, I've balanced a ten rupee coin on this tray table. If it falls, I owe you a chai. I'm your guide, and yes, I also check tickets. Please take your seats. The doors are closing.

## Coach 2

Coach two. Why is the nose so long? Because at three hundred kilometres an hour, air stops behaving like air, and starts behaving like a wall. A flat front would slam into it. A long, pointy nose slices through it instead. Tunnels are worse. A blunt train rams into a tunnel like a pump, squeezing the air ahead, and the far end goes boom. Japan fixed this by copying a bird. The kingfisher dives into water with barely a splash, so engineers gave their train a beak. Less boom. Less noise. Less electricity. Nature did the research, for free.

## Coach 3

Coach three. There's no engine up front. No diesel. No smoke. The power hangs above you. Twenty five thousand volts runs through a wire over the track. On the roof, a springy arm called a pantograph presses up against that wire, sliding along at three hundred kilometres an hour without letting go. And here's the trick. There's no single locomotive dragging everyone. Motors are spread across the coaches, so the whole train pushes together. Like a tug of war team where everyone is actually pulling.

## Coach 4

Coach four. Normal trains ride on a bed of stones. Bullet trains ride on concrete. The rails sit on solid concrete slabs, a Japanese system called J slab, used in India for the very first time. No stones to shift, so no bumps. And more than ninety percent of the route runs on a bridge, ten to fifteen metres above the ground. So no level crossings, no cows, and no uncle on a scooter trying his luck. Oh, and near Mumbai, the train dives under the sea, through a seven kilometre tunnel below Thane Creek. Under. The. Sea.

## Coach 5

Coach five. Who's driving? At three hundred and twenty, you cover almost a football field every second. So a driver can't read signals on poles outside. They'd blur past like a missed call. Instead, the signals come inside, on a screen in the cab. A system called ATC keeps telling the train the safe speed for the track ahead. Go too fast, and the train slows itself down. Politely. Without asking.

## Coach 6

Coach six. Fun fact. Bullet trains are terrible at stopping quickly. From top speed, an emergency stop still takes around four kilometres. So the computer plans every stop way ahead. And when the train brakes, its motors flip into generators, and push electricity back into the wire. Now, earthquakes. Twenty eight seismometers along the route listen for the first, faster tremor. The moment they sense it, the power is cut, and every train in the area brakes, ideally before the strong shaking even arrives.

## Coach 7

Last coach. So. A beak for a nose. Power from a wire. Motors spread across the train. Concrete track on a bridge in the sky. Signals inside the cab. And seismometers listening for earthquakes. All of that, so you can go from Mumbai to Ahmedabad faster than one long movie. And the ten rupee coin on the tray table? Still standing. I don't owe you a chai. We have arrived. Please collect your belongings. Mind the gap.
